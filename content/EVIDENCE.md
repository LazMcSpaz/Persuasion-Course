# What the research says, and the only research the course cites

A lesson may cite an entry here by its author and year, in plain words.
Nothing else is cited. No percentages or sample sizes beyond what an entry
states. If a technique has no entry, it has no controlled studies, and the
lesson says so.

## Rapport and listening

- **Chartrand & Bargh (1999), the "chameleon effect".** People unconsciously
  copy the posture and mannerisms of those they talk to, and people whose
  mannerisms were subtly copied liked their partner more and found the
  conversation smoother.
- **van Baaren and colleagues (2003).** Waitresses who repeated customers'
  orders back word for word received larger tips than those who
  paraphrased.
- **Maddux, Mullen & Galinsky (2008).** Negotiators told to subtly mimic
  their counterpart's mannerisms reached deals more often than those who
  did not. Mimicry that is noticed backfires.
- **Lieberman and colleagues (2007), affect labelling.** Putting a feeling
  into words reduced the brain's alarm response to it. The basis for naming
  an emotion you hear ("it sounds like that's been frustrating").

## Reading people

- **Bond & DePaulo (2006).** Across many studies, people detect lies only
  slightly better than chance. Reading someone's mind from their face is
  not a skill to rely on.
- **Ambady & Rosenthal (1992), "thin slices".** Brief samples of behaviour
  carry real information: snap impressions predict some outcomes. Noticing
  is useful; certainty is not.

## Language and suggestion

- **Loftus & Palmer (1974).** Asking how fast cars were going when they
  "smashed" rather than "hit" each other raised speed estimates, and later
  more people falsely remembered broken glass. Wording carries assumptions.
- **Loftus (1975).** A question that took for granted a barn that was not
  in the film ("when it passed the barn") made people more likely to report
  seeing a barn later. The cleanest evidence for presupposition.
- **Langer, Blank & Chanowitz (1978).** A request to jump a photocopier
  queue worked better with a reason attached, even an empty one ("because I
  have to make copies"). For larger requests, only a real reason helped.
- **Forer (1949), the Barnum effect.** People rate vague, general
  descriptions as highly accurate descriptions of themselves. Vague language
  feels personal.
- **Wegner and colleagues (1987).** People told not to think of a white bear
  thought of it more. Negations still put the idea in mind.
- **Lynn and colleagues, reviews of the 1990s, on indirect suggestion.**
  Laboratory comparisons of Ericksonian indirect suggestions with direct
  ones have generally found no advantage for the indirect form.
- **No controlled studies** of embedded commands, analogue marking, the yes
  set as taught, double binds as taught, nested loops, or the hypnotic
  effect of run-on sentences.

## Attention, overload and distraction

- **Petty, Wells & Brock (1976).** Distracting listeners made weak arguments
  more persuasive, because it stopped them counter-arguing, and made strong
  arguments less persuasive, because it stopped them appreciating the
  argument. Overload helps only a weak case.
- **Gilbert, Tafarodi & Malone (1993).** People under mental load were more
  likely to accept false statements as true. Busy minds believe first and
  check later, if at all.
- **Davis & Knowles (1999), disrupt-then-reframe.** Door-to-door note cards
  sold better when the price was given oddly ("300 pennies") and then
  reframed ("that's three dollars, it's a bargain"). A small moment of
  confusion followed by a clear frame raised sales.

## Freedom and pressure

- **Brehm (1966), reactance.** When people feel their freedom to choose is
  threatened, they push back, often by doing the opposite.
- **Carpenter (2013), "but you are free".** A meta-analysis of 42 studies:
  adding a phrase that reminds people they are free to say no reliably
  raised compliance with requests.

## Commitment

- **Freedman & Fraser (1966), foot-in-the-door.** People who agreed to a
  small request were more likely to agree to a larger one later. Later
  reviews find the effect real but modest and variable.

## Imagery, stories and frames

- **Gregory, Cialdini & Carpenter (1982).** Homeowners asked to imagine
  themselves enjoying cable TV were more likely to subscribe later than
  those simply told its benefits.
- **Green & Brock (2000), narrative transportation.** The more absorbed
  people were in a story, the more their beliefs moved toward the story's,
  and the less they noticed false notes in it.
- **Small, Loewenstein & Slovic (2007).** A single identifiable child drew
  more donations than statistics about millions in need.
- **Tversky & Kahneman (1981), framing.** The same outcomes described as
  lives saved or lives lost shifted people's choices.
- **Tversky & Kahneman (1974), anchoring.** An arbitrary first number pulls
  later estimates toward it. (A different thing from NLP "anchoring".)

## Questions

- **Rackham (1988), SPIN Selling.** An observational study of some 35,000
  sales calls found that in larger sales, successful sellers asked more
  questions about the buyer's problems and their consequences, and pitched
  less.
